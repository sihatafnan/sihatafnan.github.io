Who Moved the Robot? Humanoid Datasets Remember Their Operators · Sihat Afnan, Unnat Jain, Habiba Farrukh

v3 · 2026-11-24 (current)v2 · 2026-11-24v1 · 2026-11-24

1 · Anchor

video for a broad audience

Humanoid robots learn from teleoperation data. Retargeting maps every operator onto one robot body, so the data is treated as anonymous. It isn't. From the robot's joint motion alone, we recover the operator's age, gender, height, weight and identity. https://unveil-humanoid-operators.github.io 🧵

0–6 s: a person dances (motion capture). 6–12 s: the same dance retargeted onto a Unitree G1. 13–20 s: only the robot is left, and the predicted attributes appear next to the true ones. 20–26 s: a body drawn from the predicted attributes dances beside the robot. 26–30 s: closing title.

2 · Why it happens

one insight

Why? Retargeting matches the robot's pose to the human's, frame by frame. Nothing constrains how fast joints move between frames. So each operator's speed, range and rhythm pass into the robot data. Same routine, two operators: the 60-year-old's robot twists its waist 3× slower.

0–13.5 s: two G1 robots perform the same routine, dancing_routine_V001_003, at real speed. Under each robot, a live trace shows its waist speed over the last 4 s, with the running average as a dashed line. 14–21 s: the averages over the whole routine, 45.3 vs 14.0 °/s. This is the paper's Fig. 4 pair: A308 (22 yr) and A276 (60 yr). The speeds are recomputed with the paper's feature code (compute_multi_axis_features, the waist's 3 axes, 120 fps) and match Fig. 4 to 0.01 °/s.
